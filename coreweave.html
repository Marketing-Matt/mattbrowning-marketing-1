The European buyer's real question

Sovereignty

£2.5bn committed to the UK. A European headquarters in London. Two data centres live, Scotland with NVIDIA and DataVita, a sovereign deployment on GB300 aligned to the Government's Compute Roadmap, and $2.2bn across Sweden, Norway and Spain.

Almost all of the coverage of that is financial. It reaches investors and it reaches the trade press. It does not, on its own, reach the person who has to sign.

Because when a European enterprise or public-sector body buys AI compute, the question they're actually resolving isn't price per GPU-hour or time-to-cluster. It's: where does my data physically sit, who can reach it, under whose jurisdiction, what happens under a foreign legal request, and can I prove all of that to a regulator or a board risk committee.

Right now "sovereign" is a word in a press release. It converts when it becomes a set of specific, auditable claims.

That means jurisdiction stated plainly. Key custody and who holds what. Personnel access and how it's constrained. Residency guarantees with the contractual teeth to match. An audit trail a compliance lead can take into a review without translating it first.

Until that exists as marketable substance, the sovereignty story reassures people who were already going to buy. It doesn't win the ones sitting on the fence for compliance reasons — and in Europe that's a large share of the enterprise and public-sector pipeline.

This is the part I'd bring something unusual to. I run EMEA marketing at DigiCert, which means my working week is spent marketing jurisdictional trust — PKI, certificate automation, post-quantum readiness — to European security, compliance and platform leaders. The same buying committee. I know what they need to see, what makes them discount a claim, and how long it takes to move them. I'd expect to spend my first months working with product marketing and legal to turn sovereignty from an adjective into a page a CISO can forward internally.

Two motions, not one

Segmentation

CoreWeave Europe is currently hiring a VP EMEA, a Senior Director for EMEA Enterprise Business Development, a Senior Director for DACH, an EMEA Startup & VC Growth Director — and this role.

VP, EMEA Snr Dir, EMEA Enterprise BD Snr Dir, DACH EMEA Startup & VC Growth Dir Director, EMEA Field Marketing

That's a region being built from a standing start, which is the work I've done four times. It also tells you something more specific: you're standing up at least two structurally different go-to-market motions simultaneously.

Motion one
Enterprise & public sector
  • CycleNine to eighteen months, procurement and compliance gated
  • Decides onRisk, jurisdiction, reference, contractual proof
  • NeedsExecutive engagement, customer proof, analyst and policy credibility, GSI and partner cover
  • Qualified meansA named account with a budget line and a compliance path
Motion two
AI-native & VC-backed
  • CycleWeeks, sometimes days
  • Decides onAvailability, price-performance, time to first cluster
  • NeedsTechnical and developer-adjacent presence, VC and accelerator relationships, benchmarks, fast paths in
  • Qualified meansA technical evaluator with a workload and a deadline

The failure mode is running one blended programme calendar against one lead definition. It's the default, because it's simpler and because early-stage regional teams are under-resourced. Both segments get under-served: the enterprise motion slows the startup motion down, and the startup motion makes the enterprise motion look unserious to a procurement team.

I'd separate the lead definition before separating the programmes. That ordering matters — until each motion has its own definition of qualified and its own handoff, you can run two calendars and still be unable to prove which one is working.

Where the first programme goes

First 90 days

Not a brand campaign. In a market where you're new and the buyer is resolving a trust question, awareness spend is the least efficient money available — it raises recognition among people whose objection isn't recognition.

What I'd build first is small, high-intimacy and proof-led: an executive roundtable series on sovereignty and AI infrastructure, run in the markets where the data centres actually are — the UK, DACH, the Nordics, Iberia. Agenda-led, built jointly with the regional sales leads, with a credible technical or policy voice in the room who isn't a CoreWeave salesperson.

Three reasons. It's the format that moves a trust decision, because trust moves between people and not through campaigns. It manufactures the reference customers and the proof points that everything downstream depends on. And it's cheap relative to a conference programme, which matters in year one when you need evidence before you need reach.

I've just built this exact mechanic. At DigiCert I inherited a two-city executive summit — London and Frankfurt, 205 attendees, $450k — that was internally popular and no longer returning what it cost. I productised the format so it could travel, and it's now run in nine cities from Munich and Utrecht to Madrid and Johannesburg: 360 attendees, show rate up from 71% to 90%, NPS 87, on $140k. Cost per attendee down 82%, pipeline per dollar up 84%, and the programme is still running.

Before any of it, though, I'd spend the first weeks on the unglamorous part: what the pipeline actually looks like by segment, where leads are falling between marketing and sales, and whether attribution can currently tell you anything you'd bet a budget on.

What I'd need to know

The honest part

Everything above is an outside view, and outside views are confidently wrong in predictable ways. These are the questions whose answers would most change it — and the ones I'd want to spend an interview on rather than talking through my CV again.

01
How much of the sovereign story is contractually substantiated today, versus roadmap? This sets the ceiling on what marketing can claim without writing cheques the contract can't cash. It's the first thing I'd want to establish, and it changes the whole programme plan.
02
What does EMEA pipeline look like by segment — and how much is inbound off the financial-press profile versus genuinely sales-generated? A new region with a famous parent often has flattering top-of-funnel that conceals a weak enterprise motion. Knowing the split determines where the budget goes.
03
Where does the NVIDIA relationship sit commercially in-region — co-sell, co-marketing funds, joint pursuit? Partner-led is the fastest credibility shortcut available to a new entrant. I ran the EMEA GTM behind Rackspace's AWS partnership and won 70% of AWS's global marketing fund allocation that year, so I know what good looks like here and how much it's worth.
04
Is public sector a named segment with its own number, or opportunistic? Given the Compute Roadmap alignment it reads strategic, but public sector needs a different motion entirely — frameworks, procurement cycles, policy engagement. It's either a segment or it's noise, and the answer changes what this role is.
